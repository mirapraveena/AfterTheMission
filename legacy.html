What they left us

Six machines, six missions, almost sixty years. Each one answered a question that made the next mission possible.

A relay across six decades

Read from top to bottom: every explorer passed a lesson forward.

  1. 1967

    Surveyor 3

    Answered: Is the Moon's ground firm enough to land on?

    Passed on: Confidence to send astronauts, and a landing target for Apollo 12.

  2. 1969

    Eagle

    Answered: Can people land on another world and come home?

    Passed on: A proven lander design used by five more Apollo crews, and a retroreflector still used today.

  3. 1972

    Pioneer 10

    Answered: Can a spacecraft survive the asteroid belt and Jupiter's radiation?

    Passed on: Knowledge of Jupiter's environment that helped prepare Pioneer 11 and the Voyager missions.

  4. 1997

    Sojourner

    Answered: Can a wheeled robot drive on Mars?

    Passed on: The airbag landing method and the idea of a roving robot geologist.

  5. 2004

    Opportunity

    Answered: Was Mars once wet?

    Passed on: Strong evidence of ancient water, and driving and autonomy lessons used by Curiosity and Perseverance.

  6. 2021

    Ingenuity

    Answered: Can anything fly in Mars' thin air?

    Passed on: Proof that aircraft can explore other planets, shaping NASA's studies of future Mars helicopters.

Why machines left behind still matter

Some are still doing science

The laser retroreflector placed next to Eagle in 1969 still reflects laser pulses from observatories on Earth, helping measure the Moon's distance and orbit.

They show how materials age in space

Parts of Surveyor 3, brought home by Apollo 12, showed engineers how 31 months on the Moon changed metal, glass, paint and wiring.

Some are waiting for visitors

Ingenuity was programmed to keep storing daily photos and temperature readings. Future explorers who reach Valinor Hills could collect that record.

They carry our story outward

Pioneer 10's plaque is a message from Earth that will keep travelling for millions of years, long after its signal faded.

They are landmarks of history

Orbiters still photograph Apollo and Surveyor sites. These places record the first steps of exploration, like archaeological sites on another world.

Their data keeps giving

Scientists continue to study the images and measurements these missions sent home, sometimes finding new results decades later.

The next explorers are already on their way

Perseverance is collecting rock samples in Jezero Crater. Artemis is preparing to take astronauts back to the Moon. Dragonfly will fly on Titan. Every one of them stands on the work of machines that are now silent, parked or drifting. One day, some of the people reading this may visit Tranquility Base, Perseverance Valley or Valinor Hills, and see these forgotten explorers for themselves.
